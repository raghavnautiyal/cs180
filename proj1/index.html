CS 180: Project 1

Images of the Russian Empire

Overview

Sergei Mikhailovich Prokudin-Gorskii photographed many things within the Russian Empire by taking three exposures of every scene through a blue, a green, and a red filter. He recorded them as three stacked frames on a single glass plate negative. The goal of this project is to recover the color photograph from those plates.

Note: all offsets on this page are reported as (dy, dx), matching my code: dy is the vertical shift and dx is the horizontal shift.

Single-Scale Alignment

For the small JPG plates, I exhaustively search every displacement in a [-15, 15] window in both x and y. Each candidate shift is applied to the moving channel with np.roll, scored against the blue channel with normalized cross-correlation, and the best-scoring displacement wins.

Before scoring, I crop away the outer 10% of both images. The plate borders are full of black edges and scanning artifacts that do not move with the image content, and leaving them in biases the search toward aligning the border rather than the photograph.

Normalized Cross-Correlation (NCC)

I score alignments with normalized cross-correlation. Both cropped images are mean-centered and divided by their norms, and the score is the dot product of the two resulting vectors:

NCC(a, b) = ∑ (ai − ā)(bi − b̄) ‖a − ā‖ · ‖b − b̄‖

Here a and b are the two cropped channels flattened into vectors, and ā and b̄ are their means. The score lies between −1 and 1, where 1 means the channels match perfectly.

Mean-centering removes the overall brightness difference between the channels and dividing by the norms removes the difference in contrast, so the score measures how well the two channels agree in structure rather than in raw intensity. The displacement with the highest NCC score is selected as the alignment.

Multi-Scale Pyramid Alignment

The TIFF plates are about ten times larger than the JPGs, so displacements can exceed 100 pixels and brute-force search becomes far too expensive. Instead I use a coarse-to-fine image pyramid:

  • Recursively downsample both channels by a factor of 2 until the image is small.
  • Run the exhaustive search at the coarsest scale, where the displacement is only a few pixels.
  • Going up a level, double the offset and refine it with a small +/-2 pixel search.

The same NCC metric and 10% border crop are used at every level, so the wide search only ever runs on a tiny image.

Failure Case: Emir

Emir was the one clear failure. The green channel lands in a reasonable place, but the red channel is pushed far off to (141, -205), and the result has obvious color fringing.

NCC on raw pixels basically assumes the three channels look the same except for the shift, and that isn't true here. The Emir's robe is bright blue, so it shows up really bright in the blue channel and pretty dark in the red one. The bright spots in one channel don't line up with the bright spots in the other, so NCC ends up matching things that aren't actually the same part of the photo, and the correct shift never gets the best score. Comparing edges instead of raw brightness would probably fix this, since edges stay in the same place no matter what color they are.

Every other image in the dataset aligned successfully.
